# Results and discussion

XGBoost with environmental inputs achieved the lowest validation MAE, 0.01853 kg/s. On the untouched test block, MAE was 0.01837 kg/s, RMSE 0.02366 kg/s and R² 0.834. Test MAE improved by 14.3% relative to speed-only XGBoost and 17.4% relative to the cubic baseline. The direction is consistent with environmental context adding predictive information, although correlated route and seasonal effects prevent a causal interpretation.

Performance varied by operating regime. Transit MAE was 0.01728 kg/s; near-stationary MAE was 0.02107 kg/s; low-speed MAE was 0.02566 kg/s with negative within-regime R². The system should therefore treat manoeuvring/low-speed predictions cautiously. The published water-speed field has no useful variation, limiting hydrodynamic interpretation.

Supported 100 nm scenario examples produced estimated fuel differences from 0.39% to 27.25%. The large example starts from an observed low SOG while total fuel includes hotel and auxiliary loads; shorter duration can dominate higher instantaneous flow. This illustrates both the operational importance of voyage duration and the danger of interpreting an associational predictor as a control law. The values are model estimates under a fixed environmental snapshot, not measured savings.

Extending the same protocol to all three vessels produced different validation winners. Environmental XGBoost remained best for Triton and Ceto, while a cubic baseline was selected for Poseidon. Final-test MAE/R² were 0.01837/0.834 for Triton, 0.07510/0.950 for Poseidon, and 0.04570/0.579 for Ceto. Environmental XGBoost improved final-test MAE over the cubic baseline by 17.4% and 32.8% for Triton and Ceto, but was 8.1% worse for Poseidon.

Three expanding-window evaluations clarified this split dependence. Environmental XGBoost achieved the lowest mean MAE for all vessels: 0.01829, 0.06339 and 0.04437 kg/s for Triton, Poseidon and Ceto. Standard deviations were 0.00183, 0.02450 and 0.01645 kg/s. The environmental model is stable for Triton and less stable for Poseidon and Ceto. This is stronger evidence than one split, while still falling short of cross-vessel or route-independent generalisation.

The vessels have different operating profiles. Ceto contains approximately 83.5% near-stationary observations, consistent with offshore support and dynamic-positioning activity, whereas Triton is approximately 73.1% transit. This helps explain why prediction difficulty and R² differ by vessel. Comparisons should use vessel-specific absolute-error context rather than ranking vessels solely by R².

Figures in `reports/figures/` and complete values in `reports/metrics/` are generated by the executed pipelines.
